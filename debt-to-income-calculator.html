Formula

Debt-to-income ratio = monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100.

Debt-to-income calculator FAQ

Is a lower debt-to-income ratio always better?

For borrowing capacity and cash-flow resilience, lower is usually stronger because less income is committed to debt. The right target still depends on income stability, living costs, savings, and lending rules.

Should I use gross income or take-home pay?

This calculator uses gross monthly income because DTI is commonly expressed that way. For personal budgeting, also compare the payment burden with take-home pay.

Does DTI include the new loan I am considering?

Start with existing debt to understand your current position, then add the proposed new payment to see how the ratio changes.

Can this calculator tell me whether a lender will approve me?

No. Lenders also consider credit history, deposit size, assets, expenses, loan type, documentation, and local rules. Use this as a planning estimate only.

How to use DTI for planning

Use this calculator before comparing mortgage affordability, refinance options, or debt payoff strategies. Test the current position first, then model what happens if a loan is paid down, a credit card is cleared, or income changes.

What to include in monthly debt payments

  • Mortgage or rent-related loan repayments.
  • Car loans, personal loans, and student loan payments.
  • Minimum required credit-card payments, not the full card balance.
  • Any other recurring debt obligation that appears in your monthly budget.

Do not include groceries, utilities, insurance, or entertainment unless you are doing a broader household budget review.

Debt-to-income examples

Example 1: If monthly debt payments are $1,850 and gross monthly income is $6,200, DTI is 29.84%. That means about thirty cents of every pre-tax dollar is already allocated to debt obligations.

Example 2: If debt payments rise to $2,600 while income stays at $6,200, DTI becomes 41.94%. The borrower may still be able to afford some commitments, but there is less flexibility for new loan payments or income interruptions.

What your debt-to-income ratio means

Debt-to-income ratio, often shortened to DTI, compares required monthly debt payments with gross monthly income before tax. It is commonly used as a quick affordability signal because it shows how much of monthly income is already committed before new borrowing is added.

A lower DTI generally leaves more room for savings, emergencies, housing costs, and discretionary spending. A higher DTI can indicate that even a small rate increase, income change, or unexpected expense may put pressure on cash flow.
